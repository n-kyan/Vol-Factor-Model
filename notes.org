  Philosopher Stephen Toulmin’s model of argument splits it into three parts:

Claim: what you’re arguing
Grounds: the evidence
Warrant: the reasoning that connects the evidence to the claim


Analysis is the warrant. Most weak essays have a claim and evidence but leave the warrant unstated and assume the reader will make the connection. Classrooms often teach a simpler version called Claim–Evidence–Reasoning (CER).

Why it’s the important part

Evidence is interchangeable; reasoning isn’t. Anyone can find a quote. The specific way you connect it to your claim is your contribution.
It’s where the thinking is visible. Evidence shows you did research. Analysis shows you understood it.
It’s what persuades. A reader who disagrees with you will rarely dispute your facts. They’ll dispute what you claim those facts mean. If you don’t make that case, you haven’t argued anything.
It’s the only part that can be wrong. That makes it the part worth judging.

Quick ways to explain it to others

Courtroom: Exhibits don’t win cases. The closing argument that explains what the exhibits prove does.
Cooking: Evidence is the ingredients. Analysis is the cooking. Nobody serves a pile of raw ingredients and calls it a meal.
The “so what?” test: After every piece of evidence, ask “so what?” The answer is your analysis. If you can’t answer it, the evidence is decoration.

A practical rule of thumb you could pass on: if a paragraph/project is mostly quotes or data with a sentence of commentary, flip the ratio.
